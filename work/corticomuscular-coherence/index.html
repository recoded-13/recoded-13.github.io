What does bad posture cost the brain?

Corticomuscular and intramuscular coherence during balance tasks of graded difficulty, comparing forward head posture with controls.

Reehab AhmadResearch Assistant · University of Sharjah20262 min read

Problem

Forward head posture is everywhere: phones, laptops, long days at a desk. Its effects on the neck and spine are well studied. Its effect on how the nervous system controls balance is much less clear. If the head sits forward of the body’s line, does the brain have to work harder to stay upright, especially when balance gets difficult?

Approach

Corticomuscular coherence measures how strongly activity in the motor cortex and activity in a muscle oscillate together. It is a window into how directly the cortex is driving the muscle. Intramuscular coherence, between recordings from the same muscle, reflects the shared drive arriving at the motor units.

The study recorded EEG and surface EMG at the same time while participants with forward head posture and a control group performed balance tasks of graded difficulty. Grading the difficulty is the key design choice. It lets you see whether group differences stay constant or grow as the task demands more.

What I analysed

I was the data analyst on the study, working with Dr. Ibrahim M. Moustafa, Dr. Alham Al-Sharman and Dr. Meeyoung Kim.

  • Acquisition. EEG recorded on Nexus (Mind Media), with synchronised surface EMG.
  • Preprocessing. Cleaning and segmenting both signals by task condition.
  • Coherence. Corticomuscular coherence between EEG and EMG, and intramuscular coherence within muscles, across frequency bands.
  • Comparison. Group (forward head posture vs. control) × task difficulty.

Result

The headline finding is in the manuscript’s title: during the more challenging balance tasks, corticomuscular coherence points to increased cortical engagement with postural malalignment. The brain appears to take a more hands-on role in balance when posture is off.

The manuscript is under review at the IEEE Journal of Translational Engineering in Health and Medicine. I’ll add the full figures and numbers once it is published.

What I learned

Coherence is simple to compute and easy to over-read. Most of the work is upstream: getting two different signals cleanly aligned, choosing segments that mean the same thing across participants, and resisting the urge to report every band that crosses a threshold. The graded-difficulty design earned its keep here, because a difference that grows with difficulty is far more convincing than a single contrast.

This study is one of four I work on in the same programme. The others look at EEG functional connectivity, executive function, and cortical excitability and gait in people with forward head posture.
