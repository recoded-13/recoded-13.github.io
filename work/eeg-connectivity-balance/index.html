Brain networks on an unstable surface

EEG functional connectivity in forward head posture vs. controls during unstable balance tasks.

Reehab AhmadResearch Assistant · University of Sharjah20262 min read

Problem

Balance isn’t one brain area’s job. It is a conversation between sensory, motor and frontal regions that has to speed up when the ground stops being reliable. Coherence with a muscle (see J2) tells you about the cortex-to-muscle link. It doesn’t tell you how the cortex reorganises itself. For that, you look at how brain regions couple with each other.

Approach

Functional connectivity quantifies statistical dependence between EEG channels. Computed per frequency band, it gives a matrix for every participant and condition: a map of which regions are working together. The hero graphic on the home page is a stylised version of exactly this.

The study compares people with forward head posture to controls during unstable balance tasks. The question is whether malalignment changes the network the brain recruits to stay upright.

What I analysed

  • EEG acquisition and preprocessing on Nexus, segmented by balance condition.
  • Time-frequency analysis to see how band power evolves during the tasks.
  • Functional connectivity matrices per band, participant and condition.
  • Group comparisons of connectivity patterns between forward head posture and control participants.
  • Balance measures from posturography, analysed alongside the EEG.

Result

The manuscript is under review, so the findings stay under wraps for now. I’ll publish the figures and a plain-language summary here as soon as it is out.

What I learned

Connectivity analysis has a thousand knobs: the metric, the band, the reference, the threshold. Every one of them can manufacture a network. The discipline is deciding them before you look, and checking that the effect survives reasonable alternatives. Working next to a coherence study on the same participants was useful: two methods, one question, and a built-in sanity check when their stories agree.

This project sits in the same programme as a cross-sectional study linking forward head posture to executive function and cortical activity, and a comparison of cortical excitability, balance and gait in university students.
