Moving a robotic hand with three EEG channels

An end-to-end, real-time motor imagery BCI that turns imagined hand movements into robotic-hand commands on a Raspberry Pi.

Reehab AhmadFinal year project · Riphah International UniversityAug 2024 – Jun 20252 min read

Problem

Most motor imagery BCIs you read about run on dense caps, lab PCs and offline analysis. I wanted to know how far you can get with the opposite: three EEG channels, a Raspberry Pi and a hard real-time constraint. Could someone imagine moving a hand and see a robotic hand respond, reliably enough to be useful?

Approach

Motor imagery changes the power of sensorimotor rhythms over the motor cortex, and the change is lateralised: imagining your left hand shows up on the right hemisphere and vice versa. That makes a small montage over the sensorimotor strip a reasonable bet, if the rest of the pipeline is careful.

I designed the stimulus protocol in PsychoPy and treated it as something to engineer, not a fixed input. Across several pilot rounds I changed cue timing, trial structure and the wording of task instructions until participants produced motor imagery responses that could actually be told apart. The protocol mattered as much as any classifier choice.

What I built

  • Acquisition and streaming. A custom real-time streaming and processing pipeline on Lab Streaming Layer (LSL), so the recording, the stimulus and the decoder share one clock.
  • Signal processing. Bandpass filtering, then artifact removal, then Common Spatial Patterns to find spatial filters that maximise the difference between the two imagery classes.
  • Classification. Linear Discriminant Analysis on the CSP features: fast, stable and easy to run on small hardware.
  • Deployment. The decoder runs on a Raspberry Pi, which sends commands to the robotic hand with low latency.
  • Data. An EEG dataset from 30+ participants, collected with the final protocol.

Result

The system works end to end: imagined movement in, robotic-hand command out, on embedded hardware in real time.

The more useful result was a diagnosis. Offline accuracy was not what limited real-time performance. Transfer was: a decoder trained in one session or on one person degraded in the next session or on someone else. That changes where the effort should go next: towards adaptation and calibration, not squeezing another point out of offline cross-validation.

What I learned

Benchmarks reward offline accuracy, and users experience online behaviour, which is a different thing. Building the whole chain, from the words on the cue screen to the motor on the hand, showed me that the weakest link moves around. At different points it was the protocol, the artifacts and the session-to-session drift. It was rarely the classifier.
